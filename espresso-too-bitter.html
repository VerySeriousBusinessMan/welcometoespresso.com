Why Is My Espresso Bitter? 6 Causes and How to Fix Each

7 min read Updated June 2026
Quick answer

Bitter espresso almost always means over-extraction — the water spent too long in contact with the coffee grounds and pulled out harsh, unpleasant compounds. The most common cause is a grind that's too fine. Start there: coarsen your grind by one or two clicks and pull another shot.

If the grind isn't the issue, work through the other five causes below in order.

There's a difference between bitter and strong. A well-extracted dark roast can taste bold and intense without being unpleasant. What you're trying to diagnose here is the harsh, dry, ashy bitterness that makes you wince — that's always a sign something went wrong in the extraction.

01

The 6 causes of bitter espresso

Cause 01
Grind too fine

The most common culprit. When the grind is too fine, water has to push hard through the puck, spends too long extracting, and pulls bitter compounds that should stay behind. A shot that takes longer than 35 seconds is usually a sign of this.

Fix
Coarsen your grind by 1–2 notches. Pull another shot and check the time. Target 25–35 seconds for a double shot from first drop to last.
Cause 02
Extraction time too long

Even if your grind is right, if you're letting the shot run past 35–40 seconds, the tail end of the extraction will be bitter. The first half of a shot is sweet and acidic; the second half turns bitter.

Fix
Stop the shot earlier. Try ending at 28–30 seconds and taste it. Use a scale with a timer to measure consistently — see our scale picks.
Cause 03
Dose too high

Packing too much coffee into the portafilter creates excessive resistance and over-extracts the grounds. A standard double shot uses 17–19g in a 54mm basket or 18–21g in a 58mm basket.

Fix
Weigh your dose. If you're guessing, get a scale — even a small variation of 1–2g changes the shot significantly.
Cause 04
Water too hot

Higher water temperature extracts more aggressively. At 205°F+, the shot can tip into over-extraction even with a correct grind and dose. Most machines run at a fixed temperature — if yours allows adjustment, this is worth checking.

Fix
If your machine has temperature control, try 197–200°F. For dark roasts specifically, dropping to 195°F can reduce bitterness significantly.
Cause 05
Stale or low-quality beans

Coffee goes stale after roasting, and stale beans produce bitter, flat shots regardless of what you do with the grind. Cheap beans are also frequently over-roasted, which amplifies bitterness. Check the roast date — coffee is best within 2–5 weeks of roasting.

Fix
Buy beans with a printed roast date (not a "best by" date). Avoid anything roasted more than 6 weeks ago. Our bean guide has beginner-friendly picks.
Cause 06
Dirty equipment

Old coffee oils go rancid and taste intensely bitter. If your portafilter basket, group head, or shower screen has built-up residue, it will contaminate every shot you pull.

Fix
Rinse your portafilter basket after every use. Run a blank shot (water, no coffee) through the group head before pulling. Deep clean weekly with a group head brush and backflush if your machine supports it.
02

How to figure out which one it is

Work through this checklist in order:

1. Check your extraction time. If it's over 35 seconds, coarsen the grind and stop here — that's almost certainly the problem.

2. Check your dose. Are you weighing it? If not, weigh it once to see where you are. More than 21g in a standard double basket is too much.

3. Check your beans. What's the roast date? Anything beyond 6 weeks is suspect. Is the roast level very dark (French roast, Italian roast)? Dark roasts are inherently more bitter.

4. Clean everything. Rinse the basket, run a blank shot through the group head, and pull again. Sometimes the fix is this simple.

Bitter AND sour at the same time?

This is usually a grinder problem. Cheap blade grinders and low-quality burr grinders produce uneven particle sizes — the fine particles over-extract (bitter) while the coarse ones under-extract (sour), and you taste both at once. The fix is a dedicated espresso burr grinder. See our grinder guide.

03

When bitterness is normal

Not all bitterness is a defect. Dark roasts (French roast, Italian roast, anything labeled "espresso roast" at the grocery store) contain more bitter compounds by nature — that's part of their flavor profile. If you're using a very dark roast and the shot tastes bitter, try switching to a medium or medium-dark roast first before chasing a dialing problem.

Robusta beans are also naturally more bitter and caffeinated than Arabica. Many commercial espresso blends include some Robusta for crema and body — if you want lower bitterness, look for 100% Arabica on the bag.
